RUÍDO

Building a public-data observatory for Brazilian politics: traceable legislative records, regional indicators, and a deliberately punk visual language.

Next.js TypeScript Prisma SQLite GitHub Actions Open Data
Leonardo Moreira Published October 2026

Abstract

RUÍDO (Radar Congresso) is an open-source civic technology project that consolidates records from the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies, Federal Senate, Portal da Transparência, IBGE and Banco Central. Its goal is to make official information easier to inspect while refusing to fill gaps with assumptions.

Project status: Under active development and historical rebuild. Data coverage and live deployment must be checked on the project site.

1. Why a public-data project?

Brazil publishes a great deal of institutional data, but a citizen who wants to compare legislative votes, parliamentary records and public money still needs to navigate different sources with different formats and update cycles. RUÍDO began as an experiment in turning those scattered public records into something people can inspect.

Transparency requires more than a dashboard: the data must be traceable to the source.

— Design principle

The project uses an intentionally confrontational punk-collage identity, but its data rules should be conservative. It cannot claim an individual's vote when no individual record was published; it cannot turn a missing attendance record into a confirmed absence.

2. The official-data pipeline

Different institutions answer different questions. The Câmara dos Deputados and Senado Federal provide records of parliamentary activity, proposals and voting. The Portal da Transparência / CGU provides federal amendment data. The IBGE supplies regional population and economic statistics, while the Banco Central publishes financial series.

These are not interchangeable datasets. A credit stock at the central bank is not a federal investment, and an amendment paid to a location is not the same as that region's total budget expenditure. RUÍDO keeps those distinctions visible when combining indicators in the Brazil map.

Data integrity rule

Unknown is not zero. A missing individual vote is not an absence. A record that could not be safely matched to a politician remains unmatched rather than being forced onto a profile.

3. Architecture: collection without a public backend

The application is developed with Next.js, TypeScript, Prisma and SQLite. GitHub Actions performs the collection work server-side. During publication, a static export generates the pages and data files that GitHub Pages serves to visitors.

This approach avoids requiring every visitor to call restricted APIs or exposing a CGU API key in the browser. The site is static, but the data behind each publication is updated by a scheduled process.

4. Historical synchronization and checkpoints

Historical coverage is requested from January 2022 onward. Collectors work in bounded windows, storing successful month checkpoints for the legislative houses and page checkpoints for amendment imports. The intention is to resume verified work instead of restarting every historical request on each run.

Example keySyncExecution
history:v1:camara:2022-01
history:v1:senado:2022-01
history:v1:emendas:2022:page:00001

Source-specific identifiers and idempotent database operations help avoid duplicate records. Snapshots of the SQLite database are validated and retained as recovery points. A failed API request should not be mistaken for a successfully completed month.

5. Public-facing analysis

Legislative records

Parliamentarian profiles and proposal pages bring together the voting and participation records the collectors actually obtained. Voting results are distinguished from nominal roll-call information: symbolic votes often do not expose an individual choice for every member.

The Brazil map

An interactive state map compares economic activity, population, geographically identifiable amendments and selected Banco Central credit series. The interface displays each source's reference period and indicates when a value is unavailable.

Coverage report

A separate coverage page exposes the database's known gaps, imported months and unmatched records. It is designed to communicate the limits of the collection rather than suggesting a false impression of completeness.

6. What this project cannot claim

Some official votes are not individual records

Symbolic, secret and other voting modalities may lack nominal data. A result being known does not justify attributing a vote to each politician.

Rule: Publish aggregate results when provided and preserve the distinction between official counts and identifiable individual votes.

Identity matching is imperfect

Names in amendment sources do not always map neatly onto a person who served in Congress. Collective authorship requires additional care.

Rule: Preserve the original author field and leave associations blank when identity cannot be verified.

Historical coverage is in progress

A clean rebuild and backfill is underway. The public site may temporarily be unavailable or have incomplete years. The planned TSE patrimonial integration is not yet a validated source of published data in the application.

Rule: No invented figures and no claim of 100% historical coverage. The underlying collection and publishing process must pass integrity checks before production is updated.

7. Open source, visual identity and next steps

The scrapbook-like composition uses scanned-paper textures, worn stamps, chains, red warnings and acidic accents to make official records feel like something citizens can question rather than a government press release.

Technically, the next steps are increasing the coverage of official endpoints, measuring completeness rather than merely row counts, and improving verification of links between votes, propositions and people. The repository is public so the methodology can be inspected and challenged.
